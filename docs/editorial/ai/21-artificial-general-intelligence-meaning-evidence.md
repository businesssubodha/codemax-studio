# Artificial General Intelligence: What Would Count as Evidence?

Planned date (Australia/Sydney): 2026-10-30

Target keywords: artificial general intelligence

Meta description: Explore what artificial general intelligence means, why definitions differ, and how to assess claims about broad capability without confusing them with consciousness.

Artificial general intelligence, or AGI, refers broadly to AI with capabilities that generalise across a wide range of tasks. There is no single universally accepted test that settles every claim about it. When reading an AGI headline, the definition and evidence matter as much as the label.

## Generality and performance are separate questions
A system can be excellent at one task while limited elsewhere. Another may perform many tasks with uneven quality. A claim about AGI should explain both the breadth of tasks considered and the standard of performance expected.

Google DeepMind's research on levels of AGI proposes a framework for classifying capability and behaviour. It is one research approach, not a universally binding definition. Its existence illustrates why researchers need more precise language than simply saying a system is intelligent.

## Ask what the evaluation actually measures
Look for the tasks, conditions and scoring method. Were the examples new to the system? What tools and assistance were allowed? Was the result reproduced? A high score is difficult to interpret without knowing how it was obtained.

Also ask what was not measured. A test of written problem solving may not establish competence in a changing physical environment. A successful demonstration under close supervision does not establish the same ability without that support.

## Separate capability from autonomy
A system might produce a useful plan while a person executes every step. Another might use tools and make changes itself. These involve different kinds of interaction and different consequences when something goes wrong.

A report should say which form of assistance was present. Otherwise, a result achieved by a complete human-and-tool workflow may be attributed too broadly to the model alone. Understanding the whole setup makes the achievement easier to assess fairly.

## Intelligence claims do not settle consciousness
Competence at tasks and subjective experience are different questions. A system's fluent description of feelings is not, by itself, proof that those feelings are experienced. Likewise, an AGI benchmark is not automatically a consciousness test.

Keeping these questions separate allows a clearer discussion. You can investigate broad problem-solving ability without assuming a conclusion about inner experience. Avoid treating a philosophical interpretation as an experimentally established property.

## Be cautious with timelines
Predictions about when AGI will arrive depend on definitions, assumptions and uncertain future progress. Ask whether a date is a personal forecast, a company goal or a measured result. Those are different types of statements.

A useful reading habit is to rewrite the claim in concrete terms: this system completed these tasks, under these conditions, with these limitations. If that sentence cannot be filled in, the headline may be saying more than the available evidence supports.

## Keep the debate grounded
AGI is an important research idea, but you do not need to resolve it to evaluate a tool you use today. For practical decisions, examine the task, evidence and required human oversight. For broader claims, seek definitions and reproducible evaluations rather than treating the term as a certificate of universal ability.
