Searching for a website designer near me is a reasonable place to start when you want someone who understands your business and is easy to contact. But proximity alone cannot tell you whether a supplier is suited to the project. Build a shortlist around the work you need, how you will collaborate and the evidence each designer can show.

## Describe the job before collecting names
Write a short brief with your main service, target customers, current website and the problem you want to solve. Add the most important customer action: requesting a quote, booking a consultation or purchasing online. Include any deadline and who will supply the written content and photographs.

A Melbourne small business might need help clarifying its service pages and making enquiries easier. Another may need a content system that staff can manage. Both want website design, but the work and skills involved differ. A specific brief makes supplier responses easier to judge.

## Check relevant work beyond the homepage
Look for examples with a similar customer journey. Open service pages on a phone, follow the navigation and inspect how contact details are presented. An attractive homepage is useful evidence of visual judgement, but it does not show how every part of a project was delivered.

Ask what the supplier actually contributed to each example. Was it design, development, copywriting or ongoing support? If a portfolio describes business results, ask what period and measurement support the claim. You do not need confidential client data, but you do need enough context to interpret the evidence fairly.

## Agree what local collaboration means
Decide whether you need in-person meetings, Melbourne business-hour support or simply a reliable remote process. Ask who will attend project calls and who handles feedback. A nearby address does not guarantee that the person you meet will complete every stage of the work.

Set a practical expectation for updates: for example, a written progress note and an agreed review point for each stage. The frequency should suit the project. The goal is to know what needs your attention and what decision comes next without having to chase constantly.

## Use the same questions for each shortlist call
Ask how the designer will turn your brief into page structure, content and a working site. Request an explanation of the review process and how changes outside the original scope are handled. Discuss the launch checks and who will respond if a problem appears afterwards.

A useful question is: “Show me how my team would update a service description after launch.” Another is: “What happens to our existing article URLs?” These concrete scenarios reveal more than asking whether the website will be modern or SEO friendly.

## Look for a clear scope and a workable handover
Request a written list of deliverables, responsibilities, exclusions and ongoing costs. Include access to the domain and hosting accounts, the editing process and arrangements for moving away if required. Confirm what can be transferred and what depends on a particular platform.

Score the shortlist against your actual requirements using simple notes such as demonstrated, unclear or not included. Do not invent a numerical ranking that hides an important missing capability. Follow up on the unclear items before choosing a supplier.

## Choose the fit you can explain
The best outcome of the search is a decision you can justify: this supplier understands our customer journey, has relevant work, communicates clearly and has defined the scope. A low quote or a high search position is only one piece of evidence.
